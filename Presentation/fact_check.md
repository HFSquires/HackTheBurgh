## 1. Buy-now-pay-later (deferred payment credit) agreements in the UK became regulated by the FCA from 15 July 2026, including affordability checks.

Verdicts: partly supported (first), partly supported (second).

Since 15 July 2026 the FCA has regulated third-party buy-now-pay-later lending, which the law calls deferred payment credit (DPC). DPC is interest-free credit repaid in 12 or fewer instalments within 12 months. It is regulated only when the lender is a different business from the retailer. Lenders need full FCA authorisation or a temporary permission. Firms in the temporary permissions regime had until 1 July 2026 to register and have six months from the start of regulation to apply for full authorisation, so some lenders may still be operating on a temporary permission today. Before lending, firms must carry out proportionate affordability (creditworthiness) checks, including on small purchases. They must also give clear pre-contract information and support borrowers in financial difficulty. The Consumer Duty applies, and borrowers can complain to the Financial Ombudsman Service. Three things are outside the rules: BNPL provided by the retailer itself, DPC broking, and any DPC agreement entered into before 15 July 2026. The legal basis is SI 2025/859, made on 14 July 2025, and the FCA's final rules are in PS26/1 (11 February 2026). The FCA estimates that about 11 million UK adults use BNPL (10.9 million, or 20% of adults, in the year to May 2024).

Caveats: I agree with the researcher's verdict of partly supported. The date and the affordability checks are correct, but the original claim's unqualified "BNPL agreements" is too broad. Only DPC from a lender that is not the retailer, entered into on or after 15 July 2026, is regulated. Retailer-provided BNPL and older agreements remain unregulated, and DPC broking is not covered either.

The researcher's statement is accurate. It leaves out two points worth adding: broking is excluded, and the temporary-permission route means some lenders may not yet be fully authorised.

I did not read the full PS26/1 PDF. The rule details (pre-contract information, forbearance, and how "proportionate" checks may be tailored) are therefore taken from the FCA web pages and two law-firm summaries, not from the handbook text. HLC says checks apply "even for loans under £50".

On the commencement, SI 2025/859 art. 1(3) brings the remaining provisions into force at the end of the 12 months beginning 15 July 2025. The FCA and HM Treasury both give the start date as 15 July 2026.

The search found no changes or contradicting sources after July 2026, but there is no FCA data yet on how the regime has worked in practice.

## 2. Klarna reports buy-now-pay-later use (including on-time payments) to UK credit reference agencies such as Experian and TransUnion.

Verdicts: supported (first), supported (second).

Klarna has reported UK buy-now-pay-later use to the credit reference agencies Experian and TransUnion since 1 June 2022. The reporting covers Pay in 3 and Pay in 30 (Pay Later in 30 days) purchases made on or after that date, and includes purchases paid on time as well as late and unpaid ones. 2022 coverage by MoneySavingExpert also includes the Klarna Card. Klarna's current UK privacy notice (v18.3.0, published 30 September 2026, section 7.3.1) says that for these agreements Klarna sends the agencies the outstanding balance, payments made and any default "on a monthly basis until the agreement is closed". Klarna's UK customer-service page says that "making repayments on time consistently will help build a positive history". Pitch-ready wording: "Since June 2022, Klarna has reported UK Pay in 3 and Pay in 30 payments, on-time and missed, to the credit reference agencies Experian and TransUnion."

Caveats: I agree that the claim is supported. I confirmed the researcher's main points myself: the 2026 privacy notice's section 7.3.1 links TransUnion and Experian and states monthly reporting of balance, payments made and default; MoneySavingExpert and Klarna's 2022 press release give the 1 June 2022 start and include on-time payments; checkmyfile's page, updated 24 August 2026, says reporting to Experian and TransUnion continues. There are three points to watch. (1) Equifax: Klarna's current UK customer-service page ("Does Klarna perform a credit check and will this affect my credit score?", undated) says "Our credit checks are performed by the credit reference agencies TransUnion, Experian and Equifax" and "As a regulated lender, we're legally required to report Pay in 3 and Pay in 30 days payment data to these agencies". Klarna's own credit-agencies list (v2.4.0, 19 June 2025) names only Experian Limited and TransUnion International UK Limited, and the September 2026 privacy notice links only those two. ClearScore (data as of March 2026, page returned 403 to me, quoted via search) and checkmyfile also say Experian and TransUnion, not Equifax. Klarna may have added Equifax after the FCA's deferred payment credit regime started on 15 July 2026, but I found no announcement, so the pitch should name only Experian and TransUnion, or say "UK credit reference agencies". (2) "Legally required": this wording is Klarna's own. I found no primary FCA source (PS26/1) that requires BNPL firms to report to credit reference agencies, so do not say reporting is mandatory. (3) The privacy notice says "payments made", not "on-time". The phrase "paid on time" comes from Klarna's 2022 announcement and the press coverage. In 2022 the data did not affect credit scores at first; both agencies expected to include it by the end of 2022. Whether on-time BNPL payments now raise a score depends on each agency's scoring model, so the pitch should say they appear on credit files, not that they improve scores. Minor: the researcher dated the MoneySavingExpert article 1 June 2022, but its URL path is /2022/05/, so it was probably published in May 2022. The Which? and Finextra articles are both from May 2022, and I did not reread them.

## 3. Klarna UK charges a late fee for missed instalments (state the current amount and any cap).

Verdicts: supported (first), supported (second).

Klarna UK charges late fees on its buy-now-pay-later products. Under the terms for agreements made on or after 15 July 2026, when the FCA began regulating BNPL as "deferred payment credit", the fee on both Pay in 3 and Pay Later in 30 Days is 25% of the order total, capped at £6. Klarna charges at most two such fees per agreement, so one purchase can incur at most £12 in late fees. Pay in 3 Terms v1.0.1 and the Pay Later in 30 Days Deferred Payment Agreement v1.0.1 were both published on 14 July 2026. A fee can be charged if payment has not arrived 14 days after it was due on Pay in 3, or 7 days after on Pay in 30. Klarna gives notice before charging, charges no fee when less than £1 is owed, and charges no interest. For orders under £24, the 25% rule means the fee is below £6. Agreements made before 15 July 2026 remain unregulated and keep the old terms (Pay in 3 v1.10.0): £5 per late instalment on orders of £20 or more, 25% of the purchase price on orders of £20 or less, and at most one fee per instalment. The FCA does not cap the amount of BNPL late fees; it requires lenders to state the late fee before the agreement is made. Pitch-ready: "Since UK BNPL regulation began on 15 July 2026, a late Klarna Pay in 3 or Pay in 30 payment can cost 25% of the order total, capped at £6 per fee and two fees per purchase, so up to £12. Klarna's payment history is also reported to credit reference agencies."

Caveats: I read all four Klarna sources directly (Pay in 3 v1.0.1, Pay in 30 v1.0.1, Pay in 3 v1.10.0 and the terms index), and they confirm the researcher's figures. I also checked the FCA consumer BNPL page, which was not in the researcher's list.

I made three corrections to the wording.

1. "Up to £6 per late payment" should say the fee is the lower of 25% of the order total and £6, and that the limit of two fees applies per agreement, not per instalment.
2. Klarna's Pay in 3 product page still says "a maximum of one late fee per unpaid instalment" and gives no amount. For Pay in 3 this matches two fees per agreement, since only instalments 2 and 3 can be late, but the page may be stale.
3. The pitch line "late payments can also now be reported to credit reference agencies" overstates what is new. Klarna's help page says that from 15 July 2026 all Pay Later usage is reported as a legal requirement, and the new terms cover on-time, late and missed payments. My recollection is that Klarna reported to UK credit reference agencies before 2026, but I did not verify this, so the pitch should not say the reporting is new.

The FCA does not cap BNPL late fees; it only requires lenders to disclose the fee before the agreement. The "£6" sometimes attributed to Klarna in older coverage was Clearpay's and Laybuy's fee; Klarna's 2023 launch fee was £5. A firm whose credit is in the FCA's temporary permissions regime may have its terms revised when it is fully authorised, so the figures should be rechecked before the event. Pay in 6/12 and Klarna Financing are fixed-term loans under separate terms, which I did not check.

## 4. Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act 1974 makes credit card issuers jointly liable for purchases with a cash price over £100 and up to £30,000, and buy-now-pay-later purchases such as Klarna Pay in 3 are generally not covered by Section 75.

Verdicts: partly supported (first), partly supported (second).

Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act 1974 makes a credit card provider jointly and severally liable with the retailer for misrepresentation or breach of contract on any single item with a cash price of more than £100 and no more than £30,000. Since 15 July 2026, when the FCA began regulating third-party buy-now-pay-later ("deferred payment credit": interest-free, repaid in 12 or fewer instalments within 12 months), the same protection has applied to new BNPL agreements such as Klarna Pay in 3. BNPL agreements taken out before 15 July 2026 are still unregulated and have no Section 75 cover, and neither does BNPL provided by the retailer that sells the goods.

Caveats: I agree with the researcher's verdict and broadly with their wording. The original claim is half right. The credit-card half is correct. The BNPL half was accurate before 15 July 2026 but has been wrong since then for new third-party agreements.

I checked the following myself. (1) legislation.gov.uk, s.75(3)(b): Section 75 does not apply to a single item with a cash price "not exceeding £100 or more than £30,000". Both figures were substituted by SI 1983/1878. Paragraph (c), on running-account credit repaid in a single payment each period (charge cards), was inserted by SI 2010/1010. (2) SI 2025/859, Article 2: it disapplies or amends ss.55, 55C, 60, 61, 61A, 76, 77, 77A, 77B, 82, 86B, 86E, 87, 97, 97A, 98, 103, 17 and 129 for regulated deferred payment credit agreements. It does not mention s.75 or s.75A, so both still apply. (3) The FCA consumer page (last updated 6 August 2026) says regulation began on 15 July 2026 and that Section 75 "is available", the "same protection you'd have if you used a credit card". It also says pre-15 July 2026 agreements "will also remain unregulated" and that DPC "provided by that same business" is not regulated. (4) MoneySavingExpert (14 July 2026) independently confirms "items that cost over £100 but not more than £30,000" and that cover applies only to new BNPL purchases from 15 July 2026. Which? (15 July 2026) says the same, though it loosely writes "between £100 and £30,000". (5) I found no newer source contradicting this. I also found no change to the £100/£30,000 limits. HM Treasury's wider Consumer Credit Act reform is still in progress, and only unverified secondary sources describe its effect on s.75.

Wording points for the pitch. The limits apply per single item, not per basket: several £60 items do not qualify even if the total is over £100. The precise threshold is "more than £100", so an item at exactly £100 is not covered. Section 75 covers credit, not debit cards. Paying through an intermediary (for example a payment processor between the lender and the retailer) can break the debtor-creditor-supplier link and remove cover. The "full cash price even if partly paid on the card" point is standard guidance, but I did not re-verify it in this check. I did not read the FCA's PS26/1 in full. Before 15 July 2026 the original claim's BNPL statement was correct, so older articles (such as earlier Which? explainers) still repeat it.

## 5. For tax year 2026/27 the Personal Savings Allowance is £1,000 for basic-rate taxpayers and £500 for higher-rate taxpayers.

Verdicts: supported (first), supported (second).

In the 2026/27 UK tax year (6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027), the Personal Savings Allowance lets basic-rate taxpayers earn £1,000 of savings interest tax-free. Higher-rate taxpayers get £500 and additional-rate taxpayers get nothing. The allowance was set by Income Tax Act 2007 s.12B from 2016/17 and the figures have not changed since. HMRC's Budget 2026 rates annex, updated 5 November 2026, gives the same £1,000 and £500 for 2026/27 and 2027/28. People with little other income can also get up to £5,000 of interest tax-free through the 0% starting rate for savings. That band is reduced £1 for £1 by other income above the Personal Allowance, so it is lost once other taxable income reaches £17,570.

Caveats: I agree with the researcher's verdict. I independently checked the GOV.UK guidance page, the Budget 2026 OOTLAR Annex A (updated 5 November 2026), legislation.gov.uk s.12B and the Royal London adviser table, and all of them give £1,000 / £500 / nil. The GOV.UK guidance page names no tax year and shows no updated date, so the year-specific evidence comes from the OOTLAR annex. GOV.UK's 'Income Tax rates and allowances: current and past' page (updated 6 April 2026) shows the £5,000 starting-rate band but has no PSA row. The Finance Act 2026 makes a prospective change to s.12B(8) from 2027/28, but it changes definitions only, not the £1,000 or £500 amounts. Savings income tax rates rise from April 2027 (2027/28). That raises the tax on interest above the allowance, but the allowance itself stays the same. I found nothing more recent that contradicts the claim. The £17,570 threshold rests on the standard £12,570 Personal Allowance, so it does not apply to people with a reduced allowance (income above £100,000) or to Scottish taxpayers' band structure for non-savings income, although the PSA and starting rate themselves are UK-wide. In the pitch, 'basic-rate' and 'higher-rate' should be read as income tax bands, not the rate paid on savings.

## 6. Which? research has found that most Black Friday deals were the same price or cheaper at some point in the six months before Black Friday (give the most recent study and its figures).

Verdicts: partly supported (first), partly supported (second).

Which?'s most recent Black Friday analysis (press release, 25 November 2025) tracked 175 home, technology and health products on Black Friday deals at eight UK retailers (Amazon, AO, Argos, Boots, Currys, John Lewis, Richer Sounds and Very) from May 2024 to May 2025, which is six months either side of the 2024 sales. It found that 83% were the same price or cheaper on at least one occasion outside the four-week sales period (15 November to 12 December 2024), and that none was at its cheapest price of the year on Black Friday itself. Suggested pitch wording: "Which?'s latest analysis tracked 175 Black Friday deals at eight major UK retailers for a year around the 2024 sales: 83% could be bought for the same price or less at some other point that year, and none was at its cheapest on Black Friday itself (Which?, November 2025)." The most recent Which? study that gives a separate six-months-before figure is the 22 November 2022 release on the 2021 sales. Of 213 deals at seven retailers, 182 (86%) had been the same price or cheaper in the six months before Black Friday, and 208 (98%) were the same price or cheaper at some other time of year. Wording if the six-month framing is needed: "In Which?'s analysis of the 2021 sales, 86% of 213 Black Friday deals had been the same price or cheaper in the six months before (Which?, November 2022)." The 2021 release, on the 2020 sales, gave 92% of 201 deals in the six months before.

Caveats: I agree with the researcher's verdict and figures. I checked them against the Which? press releases from 2025, 2024, 2023, 2022 and 2021 and against the PA report on Nation.Cymru, and found no errors.

1. **The six-month framing.** The 2025 release gives no overall six-months-before percentage. Its 83% figure combines both sides of the sale and leaves out the whole four-week sales window, not only Black Friday itself. The 2024 release (2023 sales: 227 deals, 92% same price or cheaper at other times) and the 2023 release (2022 sales: 208 products, 98% same or cheaper in the six months before or after, 2% cheapest on Black Friday) also give no figure for before alone. Do not call 83% a "six months before" figure.

2. **Per-retailer figures in the 2025 release.** They cover different windows. Currys' 84% covers six months before and after, and Which? says Currys kept its pledge that no sale item had been cheaper in the six months before. Boots has no figure. Amazon disputed the study, saying Which? "analysed just 24 products" of its deals.

3. **What the measure means.** "Same price or cheaper on at least one occasion" can be met by a single day at that retailer's own price. It is not a cross-retailer comparison and does not mean the item was usually cheaper.

4. **Wording conflict in 2024.** The 2024 release has a headline of 92% but elsewhere says that 100% were available for the same or less. Avoid quoting it.

5. **A secondary figure to avoid.** Some secondary sources cite "85% in the six months prior" (including 70 of 78 John Lewis deals). That comes from an older study, not the latest one.

6. **Nothing newer yet.** As of 7 November 2026 I found no newer Which? study. Black Friday 2026 is on 27 November, and Which? has published its annual analysis in late November each year (22 to 26 November in 2022 to 2025). Re-check before any pitch after about 20 November 2026, because a release on the 2025 sales would replace these figures.

## 7. Lloyds Banking Group agreed to acquire Curve in 2025, and Lloyds Smart Wallet (provided by Curve UK) is a coming digital wallet with "spending rules" to choose which card is used.

## 10. How many UK adults use buy-now-pay-later (most recent reliable figure, e.g. FCA Financial Lives survey or similar), and what share have missed a payment?

Verdicts: partly supported (first), partly supported (second).

Usage (confirmed from the primary PDFs). The FCA's Financial Lives 2024 survey (fieldwork to May 2024, published May 2025) found that 20% of UK adults (10.9 million) had bought goods using unregulated buy-now-pay-later, which the FCA calls "deferred payment credit" (DPC), in the 12 months to May 2024. This was up 3 percentage points from 17% (8.8 million) in 2022. Use was highest among 25 to 34 year olds (30%), adults with low financial resilience (30%) and people in the most deprived areas (29%). In May 2024, 11% of UK adults (5.3 million) owed £50 or more on DPC and 2% (1.1 million) owed £500 or more. CP25/23 (July 2025) repeats the 20% / 10.9 million figure, and PS26/1 (published 11 February 2026) sets the final rules, which took effect on Regulation Day, 15 July 2026. The FCA has not published a newer Financial Lives figure. A more recent but non-comparable measure exists: the University of Bristol Personal Finance Research Centre and Aberdeen Group Charitable found that 18% of households used BNPL in the past six months, up from 15% in May 2025 (reported 3 March 2026).

Missed payments. There is no single official survey figure for the share of users who missed a payment. The defensible figures are these:
(a) Financial Lives 2024: 8% of DPC users (0.9 million) said they had paid a late-payment fee in the past 12 months, down from 11% in 2022. This excludes "don't know" answers. 23% of users (2.5 million) were unaware that a fee could be charged, up from 19% in 2022. The 23% is based on users who could name their provider.
(b) DPC firms told the FCA that over three million customers missed a payment in each year from 2022 to 2024, leading to over £40 million in late fees. More than 700,000 customers had debt-collection action started against them in 2024. The FCA warns that people who missed payments at several firms may be counted more than once (CP25/23, July 2025, paragraphs 80, 91 and 163).
(c) In transaction data from the four largest providers, 10% of BNPL transactions in January 2023 had at least one missed or late repayment, and 2% incurred a late fee (FCA Occasional Paper 69, July 2025).
(d) A user-level rate from the Centre for Financial Capability (January 2024) is weakly sourced: the polling firm, sample size and fieldwork dates are not given, and the press coverage disagrees about what the 22% measures (see caveats).

Pitch-ready wording: "One in five UK adults, 10.9 million people, used buy-now-pay-later in the year to May 2024 (FCA Financial Lives 2024). BNPL lenders told the FCA that more than three million customers a year missed a payment between 2022 and 2024, and 8% of users said they had paid a late fee in the past year. BNPL has been FCA-regulated since 15 July 2026."

Caveats: I confirmed the researcher's FCA usage and debt figures and the CP25/23 and OP69 figures word for word by extracting text from the FCA PDFs. I made three corrections.

(1) "Over three million customers missed a payment in 2023" is too narrow. CP25/23 gives the same figure for 2024 (paragraph 80) and says the number was over three million every year from 2022 to 2024 (paragraph 163). "Over three million a year" is more accurate. It is a count of customers across firms, not of distinct people, so it cannot be divided by 10.9 million to give a percentage of users.

(2) The Centre for Financial Capability (CFC) figure is not reliable enough to use without caveats. UKTN and Credit Connect (both dated 3 January 2024) say 22% of BNPL users missed one or more repayments in the six months to December 2023. YourMoney (same date, same charity) says instead that "around a quarter" missed repayments and that 22% were charged late fees. The age figure is also reported inconsistently: Credit Connect gives 34% of 18 to 24 year olds charged late fees, and YourMoney gives 32% of 18 to 34 year olds charged late fees. I found no source that supports the researcher's "34% of 18 to 34 year olds missed a repayment". No outlet names the polling firm, sample size or fieldwork dates. If it is used at all, say "around one in five to one in four users reported missing a repayment (Centre for Financial Capability, January 2024)" and label it a charity poll.

(3) The FLS 8% is per user over 12 months and measures paying a late fee, not missing a payment. Missed payments without a fee are not captured, so it understates missed payments.

Other notes: OP69's 10% is a share of transactions, not of users, and covers a period when few providers charged late fees. Commercial polls (Finder/Censuswide: 54% "ever used" in 2026) use a different, lifetime measure and should not be mixed with the FCA figure. A widely repeated "one in ten BNPL borrowers referred to debt collectors" figure traces to a StepChange estimate and is not a survey of UK adults. I found no 2025 or 2026 UK survey that gives a robust user-level missed-payment rate, and no newer Financial Lives release.
